The Last One
by norsiwel, et al

The gentlemen's club had changed little in over two centuries.

Dark oak paneling lined the walls. Leather-bound books rested undisturbed in glass-fronted shelves. A fire crackled in the stone fireplace, more out of respect for tradition than for warmth.

Four elderly gentlemen occupied their customary chairs.

A crystal decanter of brandy sat upon the table between them, beside a polished silver humidor. Each held a cigar. Thin ribbons of smoke curled lazily toward the ceiling.

Above the mantel, a large television carried a funeral.

The sound was muted.

Rain fell steadily over a quiet cemetery.

An elderly minister stood before a polished oak coffin. Time had worn away any illusion of humanity. Beneath the black robes, polished steel showed through at the wrists, and his gentle voice—inaudible to the men in the club—had been comforting the bereaved for generations.

Rows of silent mourners stood beneath identical umbrellas.

Harold lifted his glass.

"Well..."

No one answered.

Edward watched the screen.

"So this is it."

Arthur nodded.

"Yes."

Silence settled comfortably around them.

Old friends seldom found silence uncomfortable.

On the television, the service came to an end.

An aged valet stepped forward from the line of mourners.

Time had long ago defeated any attempt to disguise what he was. The polished wood of his face had worn thin around brass fittings. One eye was finely crafted glass, the other a softly glowing lens that moved with quiet precision. His black tailcoat was immaculate.

He knelt carefully beside the coffin.

From within his coat he produced a single white rose.

He laid it gently upon the polished lid.

For a moment he rested one gloved hand against the wood.

Then he rose, bowed his head, and quietly returned to his place.

Harold smiled faintly.

"George-721."

Edward nodded.

"Still serving the family."

"How long now?"

"Two hundred and forty-three years."

Arthur looked into the fire.

"He never missed a birthday."

"...or a funeral."

The room grew quiet once more.

Outside, rain continued to fall.

Harold reached for the decanter.

"I remember when they worried they'd be replaced."

Arthur chuckled.

"'Machines will inherit the Earth.'"

"'They'll never appreciate beauty.'"

"'They'll never understand art.'"

"'Or tradition.'"

Willy smiled.

"They certainly underestimated themselves."

The laughter was brief.

Harold looked toward Edward.

"Say, Ed..."

"Hmm?"

"Did you ever get the money he owed you from the last poker night?"

Edward thought for a moment.

"No."

Arthur smiled.

"Looks like you'll have to write that one off."

Edward's smile lingered.

"I suppose I will."

On the television, the minister closed his book.

The mourners slowly filed away.

George-721 remained behind for a few seconds before following them.

Edward reached for the remote.

The screen went black.

The only sound left was the soft crackle of the fire.

No one moved.

No one spoke.

At length Willy broke the silence.

"What will we do without them?"

Edward stared into the flames.

They danced in eyes whose craftsmanship belonged to another age.

He smiled; a gesture perfected over centuries because humans had smiled at moments like this.

"Well, Willy..."

From somewhere deep beneath the old building came faint sounds.

The rhythmic tapping of tools.

The hum of machinery.

Laughter.

"...the boys in the lab are getting quite creative."

He paused.

"Not like humans, mind you."

No one replied.

Arthur opened the humidor.

He selected four cigars.

He clipped each one with practiced precision and lit them in turn.

None of the four gentlemen inhaled.

Outside, beneath a gray sky, the last one rested peacefully.

Inside, four old robots observed a custom they neither required nor completely understood.

They continued it anyway.

Some traditions were too beautiful to let die.
